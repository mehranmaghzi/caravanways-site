Iran, Tehran: The National Botanical Garden of Iran

An autumn morning in Iran's largest botanical garden, right by the Tehran–Karaj freeway: copper-coloured cypresses, yellow tunnels of ash trees, thorny trees by the lake, and everything you need to know before you go.

Mehran

فارسی Русский Español Oʻzbekcha Türkçe

Tehran has plenty of places worth seeing, and I want to write about them one by one. The first place we're heading to is the National Botanical Garden of Iran, which many people in Tehran simply call "the Tehran Botanical Garden".

The National Botanical Garden of Iran

Where is the botanical garden?

The botanical garden is in the north-west of Tehran, next to the Tehran–Karaj freeway near the Chitgar area, on the southern slopes of the Alborz mountains. In other words, if you drive from Tehran towards Karaj, you'll pass it on the way. Work on the garden began in 1969, and today it covers more than 145 hectares. It's a really big garden; so big that if you want to see all of it, you should set aside a whole morning.

Inside the garden, the plants are grouped by their natural habitats: Iranian ones like the Hyrcanian forests of the north, the Zagros and the southern Alborz, and foreign ones like Europe, the Caucasus and the Himalayas. There's also a lake and a river, plus a Persian chahar bagh (four-part garden), a rose garden, a rock garden, a medicinal plants section and even an insect museum.

Early morning is the best time

I went by car with Kati; we were dating back then. We set off early in the morning, and that's why we managed to see a big part of the garden. In the morning the garden is quiet, the air is fresh and the sunlight comes through the leaves. The earlier you go, the fewer crowds you'll run into and the nicer your photos will be.

Autumn in the garden: a festival of colours

The botanical garden is at its most beautiful in autumn and spring. When we went it was autumn, and it felt as if every tree had picked its own colour: one yellow, one orange, one red, and next to them pines and shrubs that were still completely green. Under our feet there was a carpet of dry leaves, and above our heads a ceiling of golden ones.

One of the first trees that caught my eye was a bald cypress. Even though it looks like a pine or a cypress, unlike them it drops its leaves in autumn. Before they fall, its tiny needle-like leaves turn brick-red and orange, and from a distance they look like a big flame under the blue sky. Several of them stood side by side, each at a different stage of turning; one still yellowish-green, another completely copper.

Bald cypresses with orange and copper leaves

A little further on, I stood under a tree whose long, toothed leaves had turned golden. They looked like chestnut leaves, and the ground beneath it was covered with fallen ones.

Me, facing a tree with golden toothed leaves

The garden's paved paths run under rows of tall trees. On one of them, ash trees lined both sides of the path and their branches met above our heads. Their leaves were bright yellow, and the sunlight coming through them turned everything golden. Look up and all you see is yellow, with patches of blue sky.

A path under the yellow canopy of ash trees

Yellow ash leaves against the blue sky

Another path between yellow and brown broadleaf trees

We also stopped by the water. The garden's lake and river are among its calmest corners. On the bank stood a tree that caught my attention: a honey locust, with a cracked trunk and long, sharp thorns sticking out of its branches and even from the trunk itself. It had lost its leaves, and its bare, thorny branches in front of the blue water and the green trees on the far bank made a strange and beautiful picture.

A thorny honey locust by the garden's lake

Then we reached some narrower stone paths. On one side were firethorn bushes full of tiny red berries; so many that from a distance it looked as if someone had splashed red paint on them. On the other side were evergreen shrubs with shiny leaves, and above us a tree with yellow, hand-shaped leaves. Green, red and yellow, all in one frame.

A stone path between firethorn bushes and a yellow tree

In another corner, the ground under the trees was completely covered with small leaves and brown seed pods, with large rocks scattered here and there. Trees with small feathery leaves, yellow and green, cast their shade over the rocks, with a few evergreen cypresses beside them. Walking on these leaves, with the crunch under your feet, is one of the joys of autumn in the garden.

Ground covered with leaves and pods, with large rocks among the trees

A nice detail: next to most of the trees and shrubs there's a small sign with the plant's name. If you're into plants, these signs help you get to know a lot of new trees on a single walk.

My advice

My advice is to walk around the whole garden, and not settle for the entrance and the first few paths. Wear comfortable shoes, put a bottle of water and a few snacks in your backpack, and set off early in the morning.

I have one more tip: the botanical garden is one of the best places in Tehran for a romantic date. It's so beautiful that if you bring your crush here, you can be sure you've won their heart!

Visiting information

Best season: autumn and spring

Best time of day: early morning, right at opening time

Opening hours: every day from 9 am to 5 pm. Closing time may change from season to season, so to be safe, plan your visit to finish by 5 pm.

Address: Tehran–Karaj freeway, Peykanshahr exit, Sarv-e Azad township (near Chitgar)

How to get there

Metro: get off at Vardavard or Iran Khodro station and take a taxi for the rest of the way.

Bus: the nearest stop is Vilashahr-e Shomali.

By car: from the Tehran–Karaj freeway, take the Peykanshahr exit. The garden has free parking. I went by car myself and it was the easiest way.

Ride-hailing taxi: the simplest option if you don't have a car and want to get there early in the morning.
