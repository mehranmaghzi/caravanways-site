From Panjakent to Bukhara: An Uzbekistan Travelogue (Part One)

A backpacker crosses overland from Panjakent in Tajikistan into Uzbekistan: a brief night in Samarkand, the Samanid Mausoleum, Po-i-Kalyan square, Bukhara's tiled madrasas, and the traces of Rudaki and the Persian-speaking poets of this land.

Mehran

فارسی Русский Español Oʻzbekcha Türkçe

A trip full of the unexpected

Uzbekistan was one of those trips full of unexpected events and hard decisions, the very things that in the end made it one of a kind. On this trip I was invited to classes at Fergana University, met two wonderful teachers there, and a friendship began that still goes on today.

I saw most of Uzbekistan's sights and walked the back alleys of Samarkand, Bukhara, Khiva, Tashkent, Fergana and Urgench. But this part of the travelogue is about the first days: from the Panjakent border to the old city of Bukhara.

Crossing overland from Panjakent

I came from Tajikistan with my backpack, crossing overland into Uzbekistan from the town of Panjakent in the middle of Tajikistan. The border is not far from Samarkand, and very soon we found ourselves in a city whose name we had heard for years in books and poems.

A short night in Samarkand

Our first city was Samarkand. We arrived at night and walked its streets for a little while, just enough to get a feel for the place. Our plan was to see Bukhara first, then come back to Samarkand and finish in Tashkent. So we left Samarkand for later and took the train to Bukhara.

Bukhara's Ark and the alleys of the old town

Bukhara is one of the oldest cities in Central Asia, more than 2,500 years old. It was once an important stop on the Silk Road, and in the 9th and 10th centuries it became the capital of the Samanids. In that era Bukhara grew into one of the greatest centres of learning and literature in the Islamic world, where scholars such as Avicenna studied. Today the historic centre is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

In Bukhara we first went to see the city's beautiful Ark, a fortress with high mud-brick walls that for centuries was the home and seat of the emirs of Bukhara. Today the Ark holds several museums, and from the top of its walls there is a good view over the old town. Then we wandered the alleys of the old city and saw plenty of other sights. As always, we slept in hostels at night: cheap, simple and full of travellers from every corner of the world.

The Samanid Mausoleum

One of the places in Bukhara that touched me most was the Mausoleum of Ismail Samani. It is a small cube of brick, more than a thousand years old, and one of the oldest Islamic buildings in Central Asia. There are no tiles and no colour; all its beauty lies in the way the bricks are laid, so that as the sun moves through the day, the patterns on its walls change. For us Iranians the building means something more: the Samanids were the dynasty under whom the Persian language came back to life at court, and Rudaki was writing poetry in their time.

Getting there: the mausoleum is in Samanid Park, on the western side of the old town. It is about a ten-minute walk from the Ark and about twenty minutes from Lyabi-Hauz.

The Mausoleum of Ismail Samani: a thousand years of brickwork without a single tile

Po-i-Kalyan and the Mir-i-Arab Madrasa

When evening came I made my way to Po-i-Kalyan square, the historic heart of Bukhara. Facing the Kalyan Mosque stands the Mir-i-Arab Madrasa with its turquoise domes, a madrasa that still has students and still holds classes. The orange light of sunset fell on the bricks and tiles as the square slowly emptied.

Getting there: Po-i-Kalyan is a few minutes' walk from the Ark, and about ten minutes from Lyabi-Hauz through the covered bazaars (the trading domes). The Kalyan Minaret, the Kalyan Mosque and the Mir-i-Arab Madrasa all stand around this square.

Me in front of the Mir-i-Arab Madrasa, Po-i-Kalyan square

From under the arches of the Kalyan Mosque, the sun setting behind the city's brick walls was a sight to see. Traders were slowly packing up their stalls and people were heading home through the lanes.

Sunset in Bukhara, seen from under one of the old town's arches

The streets of old Bukhara in the evening light

Madrasas: a city that was once Central Asia's university

Bukhara is also called the "city of madrasas". Every few steps another tiled portal appears in front of you. One of the most beautiful is the Ulugh Beg Madrasa, built by Timur's grandson, the famous astronomer. Above its portal is written: "The pursuit of knowledge is the duty of every Muslim man and woman." We went early in the morning, when the square was almost empty and only a little boy was playing in front of the portal.

Getting there: the Ulugh Beg Madrasa stands directly opposite the Abdulaziz Khan Madrasa, next to the Toqi Zargaron trading dome, just a few minutes' walk from Po-i-Kalyan.

The Ulugh Beg Madrasa in Bukhara on a quiet morning

One morning, by another madrasa, the previous night's rain had left a puddle on the paving stones, and the tiled portal and the bare tree in front of it lay upside down in the water.

A madrasa reflected in a rain puddle, Bukhara

How to get around Bukhara

The good thing about Bukhara is that almost all its sights are in the old town, close to one another, and you can see them all on foot. For a backpacker, the best move is to find a hostel around Lyabi-Hauz; from there everything is within walking distance. Bukhara's railway station is actually in the town of Kagan, about fifteen kilometres away, so you need a taxi or a bus to reach the old town. Between Bukhara and Samarkand there are both trains (including the high-speed Afrosiyob) and shared intercity taxis.

Bukhara and Samarkand: a history shared with Iran

For an Iranian, walking through Bukhara feels like turning the pages of your own history book. For centuries Bukhara and Samarkand were part of Greater Iran and its cultural world. Under the Achaemenids this land, called Sogdia, was one of the satrapies of the Persian Empire, and later it stayed under the Parthians and the Sasanians. The language of its people, Sogdian, belonged to the Iranian family of languages.

But the high point of this bond came under the Samanids. The Samanids, an Iranian dynasty that made Bukhara their capital, turned Persian back into the language of court, poetry and government in the 9th and 10th centuries. Many historians see the Persian (Dari) we know today as a child of this very era and these very cities.

Great poets lived in these two cities and wrote in Persian:

Rudaki, called the father of Persian poetry, was born in the village of Panjrud near Panjakent, the very town I crossed into Uzbekistan from. He was a court poet of the Samanids in Bukhara, and he wrote his famous poem about this city: "The scent of the Muliyan stream is coming / the memory of a kind friend is coming."

Daqiqi, a Samanid court poet who began writing the Shahnameh before Ferdowsi; Ferdowsi kept a thousand of his verses in his own Shahnameh.

Am'aq of Bukhara, born in Bukhara and poet laureate at the Karakhanid court in Samarkand.

Suzani of Samarkand and Rashidi of Samarkand, poets of 12th-century Samarkand.

Nizami Aruzi of Samarkand, author of the "Four Discourses", one of the masterpieces of Persian prose.

Avicenna also studied in Bukhara and spent his youth in the great library of the Samanid emirs. And centuries later in Shiraz, when Hafez wanted to name the most precious cities in the world, he named these two: "If that Shirazi beauty would take my heart in hand / for the mole on her cheek I would give Samarkand and Bukhara."

Persian manuscripts in the Bukhara museum

In the Bukhara museum we saw many Persian manuscripts. Seeing so many Persian books in the heart of Uzbekistan was a strange feeling: a reminder of the old bond between Iran and Uzbekistan, from a time when Persian was the language of learning, literature and government in this very land.

A night taxi to Samarkand

After exploring Bukhara, we took a taxi to Samarkand at night: the city we had only glimpsed on that first evening, and which it was now time to get to know up close.
