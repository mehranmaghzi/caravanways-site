Samarkand, the City That Changed My Route (Uzbekistan Travelogue, Part Two)

Registan, Shah-i-Zinda, Bibi-Khanym and Ulugh Beg's observatory, and meeting two teachers from Fergana University who made me cancel my flight and stay in Uzbekistan.

Mehran

فارسی Русский Español Oʻzbekcha Türkçe

From Bukhara to Samarkand, this time by road

After Bukhara we arrived in Samarkand and spent the night there. We had already tried the trains in Uzbekistan, and now we wanted to see what travelling by road was like. To be honest, the roads weren't great, but they weren't bad either. More than the roads, what mattered were the drivers: warm, friendly people who played the songs we liked all the way.

I wasn't travelling alone; I had come overland from Tajikistan into Uzbekistan with two travel companions.

Samarkand, the jewel of the Silk Road

Samarkand is one of the oldest living cities in the world, around 2,750 years old. The Greeks called it Maracanda, and Alexander the Great passed through it. For centuries it was one of the most important cities of Sogdia and the Silk Road, and in the 14th century Timur made it the capital of his empire, bringing architects and artists from all over the world to build the most beautiful city on earth. Today it is on the UNESCO World Heritage List as "Samarkand – Crossroad of Cultures".

Registan Square

The heart of Samarkand is Registan Square: three huge madrasas covered in turquoise and lapis tiles, facing one another. On the left is the Ulugh Beg Madrasa, in the middle the Tilya-Kori Madrasa, and on the right the Sher-Dor Madrasa, with tigers chasing deer across its portal. The first time you stand in front of this square, you spend a few minutes just looking, without saying a word.

Registan Square: the Ulugh Beg, Tilya-Kori and Sher-Dor madrasas

Inside the madrasa courtyards, the cells where students once lived and studied are now artists' workshops and handicraft shops.

The courtyard of one of the Registan madrasas

In a corner of one of the madrasas you could dress up in traditional Uzbek clothes and have your photo taken. Brightly coloured ikat fabrics against all that tilework made a picture that took you back a few centuries.

An orange ikat robe among the tiles of the Registan

Getting there: the Registan is in the city centre, within walking distance of most old-town hostels. The ticket is for going inside the madrasas; you can also see the square for free from behind the railings.

Meeting Dilnoza and Gulsora

One of the most important moments of this trip happened in Samarkand: meeting Dilnoza and Gulsora, two language teachers from Fergana University who were incredibly kind to us. Both spoke English, Russian and Uzbek fluently, which made communicating so much easier.

We met at the mausoleum of Islam Karimov, the first president of Uzbekistan. We heard they were also going to the mausoleum and suggested we go together. From there we walked and talked, and that day we visited several of Samarkand's sights side by side.

The columned gallery of Islam Karimov's mausoleum

If you'd like to read the story of how we met in Dilnoza's own words, read Dilnoza's travelogue here.

Getting there: the mausoleum is next to the Hazrat Khizr Mosque, on a hill between the Bibi-Khanym Mosque and Shah-i-Zinda, across from Siab Bazaar. From the top of the hill there is a fine view of Bibi-Khanym and the snowy mountains behind the city.

The Bibi-Khanym Mosque seen from Hazrat Khizr hill

Shah-i-Zinda, a lane of turquoise tiles

Shah-i-Zinda is a group of mausoleums built on both sides of a narrow, stepped lane. Qusam ibn Abbas, a cousin of the Prophet, is said to be buried here, and the name "Shah-i-Zinda" ("the Living King") comes from the legend that he lived on. The higher you climb, the more colourful and delicate the tiles become.

The entrance to the Shah-i-Zinda complex

The steps of Shah-i-Zinda

The tiled lane of Shah-i-Zinda

One of the portals of Shah-i-Zinda

Getting there: Shah-i-Zinda is a few minutes' walk from Islam Karimov's mausoleum and about twenty minutes from the Registan.

The Bibi-Khanym Mosque at sunset

After his campaign in India, Timur ordered the largest mosque in the Islamic world to be built in Samarkand, and it was named after his wife, Bibi-Khanym. Its huge portal still takes your breath away, especially when the setting sun breaks out from behind it.

Bibi-Khanym and the snowy mountains behind Samarkand

The Bibi-Khanym Mosque at sunset

Sunset by the Bibi-Khanym minaret

Getting there: from the Registan, walk about ten minutes up the pedestrian Tashkent Street to Bibi-Khanym. Siab Bazaar is right next to the mosque.

Ulugh Beg's Observatory

Ulugh Beg, Timur's grandson, was more an astronomer than a king. The observatory he built in Samarkand in the 15th century was one of the largest in the world in its day, and the Iranian mathematician Jamshid al-Kashi worked there. The museum next to the observatory tells the story of Ulugh Beg and his colleagues with life-size figures.

Ulugh Beg and his colleagues in the observatory museum

Getting there: the observatory is about three kilometres north-east of the centre; it's best to take a taxi.

A hard decision

That night in Samarkand, after I parted from Dilnoza and Gulsora, I sat down with my two travel companions and we talked. After a long, heavy discussion, not without a bit of a fight, I decided to cancel my flight to south-western Turkey, which was meant to be the next day. It meant the money for the plane ticket, which was not a small amount, would not come back. But I preferred to stay in Uzbekistan and explore more of the country.

I told my friends my decision that same night in Samarkand. The next day in Tashkent I saw Dilnoza and Gulsora again and told them I wanted to come with them to Fergana.

I wanted to continue my trip with these two dear teachers to Fergana and hear about their region in detail, from them. Local sources are always far better than internet sources: they can show you places nobody may ever have written about and give you information you won't find anywhere.

My other reason was Tajikistan. There I learned that I should either not travel a country at all, or travel it properly and as fully as I can, and really understand it. Even now, as I write this, I still regret not seeing Tajikistan fully, and I hope one day to go back and explore it to my heart's content. Maybe that day I'll cross overland into Uzbekistan again too, but this time not as a traveller: to see the friends I made on this trip.
