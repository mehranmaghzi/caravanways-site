Tajikistan: Nowruz in Iran's Old Home

Nowruz 2024 in Tajikistan with a backpack and a private taxi: Dushanbe full of Persian poetry, snowy Iskanderkul lake, samanu and a Nowruz concert in Panjakent at the mayor's invitation, and the trip that made me proud to be Iranian for the first time.

Mehran

فارسی Русский Español Oʻzbekcha Türkçe

Tajikistan is one of those trips that still brings tears to my eyes whenever I think about it. Maybe for me, an Iranian, travelling there during the Nowruz holidays meant much more than it would for a foreign traveller. The beauty of it was that we got to see Nowruz celebrated in a country that was once part of Greater Iran; a country whose people speak Persian, consider Rudaki and Ferdowsi their own, and celebrate Nowruz with all their heart.

A trip that started late

There were three of us: me and two of my friends, who later split from me in Uzbekistan and went on to Turkey. My travel style is always backpacking; I travel with just a backpack and sleep in hostels, so that by saving money I can travel more. Tajikistan was no different.

The trip was supposed to last 9 days, but our visa didn't arrive before the flight. We had applied for an online (electronic) visa, not a sticker visa in the passport. If we had gone for the sticker visa, it would have arrived sooner and we could have stayed longer in Tajikistan. The trip was postponed, we lost the money for one plane ticket, and five days later we set off again. In the end we only had 4 days in Tajikistan: we started in Dushanbe, the capital, saw Iskanderkul lake on the way, reached Panjakent, and from there crossed overland into Uzbekistan.

Tajikistan and Iran: a shared history

To understand why an Iranian feels so at home in Tajikistan, we need to go back a little.

Under the Achaemenids, about 2,500 years ago, Cyrus the Great added the lands of Sogdia and Bactria to the Persian Empire. A large part of today's Tajikistan is that old Sogdia and Bactria, and the name Sughd still survives as one of Tajikistan's northern provinces. Under the Parthians and Sasanians, too, these lands were part of the Iranian cultural world, sometimes directly ruled and sometimes as close neighbours.

But the most important era for the Tajiks is the Samanid period (9th and 10th centuries, roughly 819 to 999 AD). The Samanids ruled from Bukhara and Samarkand, and it was in this period that New Persian (Dari) once again became the language of poetry and government. Rudaki, the father of Persian poetry, wrote at the Samanid court, and Ferdowsi began composing the Shahnameh in this same atmosphere. The Tajiks consider Amir Ismail Samani the founder of their nation; their currency is called the somoni and his portrait is on their banknotes.

After the Samanids the region was ruled by the Ghaznavids, Seljuks, Khwarazmshahs and Timurids; dynasties whose language of court and literature was Persian. From the 19th century the Russians arrived in Central Asia, and in the Soviet era Tajikistan became one of the Soviet republics. Finally, in 1991, Tajikistan became independent.

The Silk Road and the Pamir Highway

Tajikistan sits right on the Silk Road, a network of caravan routes that for more than a thousand years connected China to Iran and the Mediterranean. Caravans brought silk, spices, paper and porcelain, and with them travelled languages, religions, art and stories. The Sogdians, for whom Panjakent was an important city, were the great merchants of this road, and for centuries Sogdian was the language of trade along the Silk Road. Samarkand and Bukhara, now in Uzbekistan, were also major stops on this route.

Today one of the world's most famous road trips also runs through Tajikistan: the Pamir Highway (the M41). It starts in Dushanbe, runs along the Panj River and the Afghan border, passes through Khorog, and crosses passes above 4,000 metres, such as the Ak-Baital Pass at about 4,655 metres, before reaching Osh in Kyrgyzstan. Many travellers drive it by car, motorbike or even bicycle, and call it one of the most beautiful and toughest roads in the world. We didn't have time for it on this short trip, but the Pamirs are the first destination of my next trip to Tajikistan.

Dushanbe: a capital that thinks in Persian

Dushanbe takes its name from a market that was once held in this village every Monday (dushanbe means Monday in Persian). Today it is a city of wide avenues, big parks and monuments, most of them tied in some way to Iranian history and culture. We arrived in Dushanbe at Nowruz, and the city was drowning in lights and Nowruz decorations.

Independence Square

One of the first places we saw was the gate of Independence Square. Above it, in Cyrillic letters, is written "Майдони Истиқлол"; read it aloud and it says "Meydan-e Esteghlal", exactly the same words we use in Persian. This was one of the first moments I realised that behind these Russian letters, our own language is hiding.

The gate is a huge white stone arch with tall columns and blue tiled domes, mixing the feel of Iranian and Central Asian architecture with a classical European style. The square was built to commemorate Tajikistan's independence from the Soviet Union in 1991, and major national celebrations are held there. We arrived at sunset during the Nowruz days. The sky had turned pink and blue, the illuminated trees were glowing, and in the middle of the square stood a tall, multi-tiered green and colourful structure, like a giant Nowruz cake. People strolled calmly and took photos, and we were lost in watching.

The gate of Independence Square (Майдони Истиқлол) in Dushanbe, at sunset during Nowruz with lights and decorations

Another thing you notice early in the city is Dushanbe's giant flagpole, about 165 metres tall, which was once the tallest flagpole in the world, with the red, white and green flag of Tajikistan waving at the top.

The flag of Tajikistan on Dushanbe's giant flagpole

Rudaki, Ferdowsi, Avicenna and Cyrus

Wherever you go in Dushanbe, you meet a poet or a great figure of Iranian culture. In the middle of Rudaki Park, the statue of Rudaki stands beneath a large mosaic arch full of stars, suns and trees of life.

The Rudaki monument, honouring the father of Persian poetry, in Rudaki Park, Dushanbe

On the wall of one of the city's buildings, statues of Avicenna (Ibn Sina), Rudaki and Ferdowsi sit side by side, and below them, on books carved in stone, are verses by Jami, Rudaki, Khayyam, Ferdowsi, Saadi and Hafez, written both in Tajik Cyrillic and in our own Persian script. "Knowledge is a bright lamp in the heart" by Rudaki, "I laid the foundation of a lofty palace of verse" by Ferdowsi, and "Plant the tree of friendship, for it bears the heart's desire as its fruit" by Hafez. We came across this wall suddenly, while walking through the city centre. Standing in front of it and reading poems I had grown up with since childhood, in another country, was a strange feeling.

Avicenna, Rudaki and Ferdowsi, with verses by Persian poets in both Cyrillic and Persian script

At night, the statue of Cyrus the Great shone under the lights in one of the city's parks. The plaque on its pedestal says in Tajik that he was the founder of the first great Achaemenid empire and the author of the first declaration of human rights.

The statue of Cyrus the Great (Kurushi Kabir) in Dushanbe

We also visited the National Museum of Tajikistan in Dushanbe, a large museum that tells the story from ancient times to today. In the museums there were also old manuscripts of poetry books by the great Persian-speaking poets of those times. Seeing collections of poems handwritten centuries ago in this very land meant a lot to me.

One of the most fascinating parts of the trip was listening to Tajik guides and experts in the museums. They proudly talked about the time of Cyrus, when all these lands were one with Iran and together we were the superpower of the world.

Embroidered Tajik skullcaps (toqi) in the National Museum of Tajikistan

The statue of a writer on a bench, on one of Dushanbe's streets

Dushanbe's parks are worth seeing too: clean and full of flowers, with lamps shaped like peacocks spreading their tails. With Nowruz here, the flowerbeds were full of pansies and tulips.

A peacock lamp and a flowerbed in one of Dushanbe's parks

The bazaar and Tajik money

We also visited Dushanbe's biggest bazaar, a market with a large round roof. As on every trip, the bazaar was where you could see people's real lives: narrow aisles full of bags, atlas silk fabric, clothes and knick-knacks, and sellers who smiled when they heard us speaking Persian.

The busy aisles of the bazaar

Tajikistan's currency is the somoni, and each somoni is 100 diram. The 100-somoni banknote shows Amir Ismail Samani and his mausoleum in Bukhara. Ismail Samani (849 to 907 AD) was the most powerful of the Samanid emirs; his capital was Bukhara, and under him these lands became united and prosperous and the Persian language gained new life. For the Tajiks he is the father of the nation. The country's highest peak is named after him, Ismoil Somoni Peak, and in central Dushanbe, on Dousti (Friendship) Square, his large golden statue stands beneath a tall arch.

A 100-somoni banknote with the portrait of Ismail Samani, and two 50-diram coins

Tajik Persian: the same language, a little different

The Tajiks spoke Persian with us, but their Persian was a bit different from ours. For example, we call a photo "aks", but they call it "surat". Instead of "khub" for good, they say "naghz", an old word we know from Persian poetry but rarely use today. And instead of "merci" for thanks, they say "rahmat". It was full of words like these. In my opinion, the Persian of Tajikistan is more untouched and more original than the Persian of us Iranians.

Another interesting thing was how seriously they take Persian poetry. Everywhere in the city you saw Persian verses; something that gets less attention in Iran itself.

Iskanderkul: a lake among the snow

To get from Dushanbe to Panjakent, the three of us hired a private taxi. Our driver was Khurshid, and we had a great time with him. We spent three days and two nights with Khurshid; he slept separately at night and in the morning drove us around again, until he finally dropped us in Panjakent. The whole three days cost just 50 dollars for the three of us!

On the way we stopped at Iskanderkul lake. Iskanderkul lies in the Fann Mountains at about 2,200 metres and is named after Alexander the Great (Iskandar); "kul" means lake in the languages of the region. Legend says that Alexander's beloved horse, Bucephalus, drowned in this lake.

When we arrived, the lake was snowy and endlessly beautiful. The mountains were covered in snow, clouds sat on the peaks, and the water had a colour somewhere between blue and grey. I will never forget the silence there.

Iskanderkul lake among the snowy Fann Mountains

A tree-lined path by Iskanderkul lake

Panjakent and Nowruz: the heart of the trip

Panjakent is an old town in western Tajikistan, near the Uzbek border and about 60 kilometres from Samarkand. The ruins of ancient Sogdian Panjakent can still be seen next to the modern town, and Rudaki's mausoleum is in the village of Panjrud, near the town.

This is where we saw Nowruz in its true sense. We ate samanu and saw how it is made; the Tajiks call it "sumalak". Women gather around huge cauldrons and stir it all night long while singing. The Nowruz spreads were full of sprouted wheat (sabzeh), big breads, sweets and fruit.

A Tajik Nowruz spread with sprouted wheat, bread, sweets and fruit; that year Nowruz fell in the middle of Ramadan

And most importantly, at the invitation of the mayor of Panjakent, we joined their Nowruz celebration in a hall. The mayor was at the town's Nowruz ceremony, and besides us many other Iranians had come with tours. He invited all the Iranians and told Khurshid, our driver, to bring us to the hall. Khurshid showed us the way. Entry to this celebration was free for Iranians and Tajiks. Music, dancing, colourful clothes and people's joy… we enjoyed every moment.

Right there, in the middle of that celebration, I swore to myself that I would come back to Tajikistan; this time to stay longer than 4 days and see every corner of this land.

The Nowruz celebration in a hall in Panjakent, to which the mayor invited us

Tajik bread and food

One strange thing we saw was their bread, the same kind I later saw in Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan too. Tajik bread (non) is round and thick, with a raised rim and a thin centre covered in tiny patterns stamped into the dough with a special stamp. It is baked in a tandoor oven and treated with great respect: they never put it upside down, never throw it away, and break it into pieces by hand at the table. If you are a guest in a Tajik home, the first thing they put in front of you is this bread.

Their food was really delicious too. "Osh", or plov, is their national dish: rice with yellow carrots and meat, cooked in huge cauldrons. "Qurutob" is a dish unique to the Tajiks: pieces of flaky fatir bread soaked in qurut (dried yoghurt) sauce, topped with fried onions and herbs, eaten by hand by everyone around one big wooden platter. Tandoor-baked sambusa, shurbo soup, lagman noodles and manti dumplings are found everywhere as well.

Their kebabs were excellent. On the road we also shared a whole roast chicken between the four of us, together with Khurshid, and it tasted amazing; maybe it stood out so much because we were so hungry.

Ramadan in Tajikistan

We went to Tajikistan during Ramadan. People were fasting, but you could still eat in the streets and restaurants. It wasn't like Iran, where everything closes and eating in public is forbidden. During this month bars and cabarets were closed, but you could buy alcohol in supermarkets. One important tip: it's better not to smoke in public in the city; near the airport there were even "No smoking" signs.

People, colours and respect for Iranian culture

The president of Tajikistan has a strong interest in ancient Iranian culture and has asked people to speak Persian, be joyful, dance and wear colourful clothes. Because of this, in the city we saw many women and girls in beautiful atlas silk dresses in cheerful colours; clothes that made the city far, far more beautiful. The Nowruz celebrations I experienced in Tajikistan were much more captivating than the Nowruz ceremonies I had seen in Iran.

Central Asia really is on another level. Its countries are amazing and full of life, and its people are warm, hospitable and lovable. I had this feeling in Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan too; their culture is very close to ours as Iranians.

A beautiful country, still on the road to development

Tajikistan is a mountainous, cold country with lots of water; more than 90 percent of its land is mountains, and it has many rivers and glaciers. But economically it is not very advanced and counts as a developing country.

Tajikistan's total gross domestic product (GDP) is about 14 billion dollars, and GDP per capita is around 1,300 to 1,400 dollars a year; one of the lowest in Central Asia. Nearly half of the country's economy depends on money that Tajik workers in Russia send home to their families. The monthly minimum wage has risen to about 1,000 somoni (around 110 dollars) since September 2025.

The first time I was proud to be Iranian

Tajikistan is one of those countries where, after coming back to Iran, I kept thinking about packing up and moving there. The respect that the people and the government had for the culture of ancient Iran made me feel proud to be Iranian for the first time.

I know that where we are from and where we were born is not our choice and has no value in itself. I grew up with the Shahnameh and Persian literature since childhood, in an artistic family, but because I felt that in my own country Iranian culture and ancient history were not given the importance they deserve, I didn't have good feelings about my country. Since this trip, I am proud of Iran's ancient history. I hope a day comes when, as in the days of Cyrus and Darius, the world enjoys more peace and Iran is a powerful country.

Next Nowruz, Tajikistan again

From Panjakent we crossed the border overland into Uzbekistan. But Tajikistan is now one of my main destinations for Nowruz trips. If money and time, and my country's economic, political and wartime situation allow it, I will definitely go to Tajikistan again for Nowruz and cross overland into Uzbekistan once more.

Useful information

Visa: Iranians need a visa for Tajikistan. Our online visa arrived late; a sticker visa is issued faster. Whichever you choose, apply early so you don't lose a plane ticket like we did.

Accommodation: We slept in hostels. You can check hostel prices in Dushanbe and Panjakent on Booking.com.

Getting around: A private taxi for three people from Dushanbe to Panjakent, including Iskanderkul, for three days and two nights, cost 50 dollars in total.

Money: Somoni (TJS), 1 somoni = 100 diram.

Best time to see Nowruz: around 20 to 25 March.

Our route: Dushanbe → Iskanderkul → Panjakent → land border to Uzbekistan (Samarkand).
