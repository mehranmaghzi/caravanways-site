The Trip That Was Never Planned: From Bishkek to Issyk-Kul

A backpacker on his way home to Iran through Lebanon and Syria ended up in Kyrgyzstan instead: book-reading passengers, Bishkek's hospitality, nomad yurts, the red rocks of Jeti-Oguz and a lake that never freezes.

Mehran

The trip that was never planned

This travelogue is about northern Kyrgyzstan: Bishkek and the area around Lake Issyk-Kul, the second-largest mountain lake in the world.

To be honest, I never meant to go to Kyrgyzstan. My plan was something else entirely: from Turkey to Lebanon, from Lebanon into Syria, then Iraq, and from there overland back home to Iran. But the moment I landed in Istanbul, the news came that Israel had struck Beirut. My plans fell apart overnight and I had to change course, this time towards Kyrgyzstan.

I'm a backpacker and I always travel with just one backpack. I sleep in hostels, travel cheap, and more than anything I love talking with local people. The problem was that I had packed my backpack for somewhere else. The clothes in it were made for the heat of Beirut and Syria, not for the cold mountains of Kyrgyzstan. But that's travel: sometimes you just have to set off with whatever is in your backpack.

For Iranians, entering Kyrgyzstan by air is possible with a visa on arrival at the airport, but to enter overland you need a visa sticker in your passport.

A plane full of books

On the flight from Istanbul to Bishkek I saw something that really struck me. For the whole flight, most of the Kyrgyz passengers had a book in their hands and were reading. During those five hours it felt like sitting on a plane full of book lovers. And whatever I asked the people next to me, they answered patiently and seemed glad to chat with a stranger.

My first Kyrgyz friend

When I arrived at Bishkek airport, I was looking for a cheaper way than a taxi to get into the city. I asked a young Kyrgyz guy how the city buses worked. As it happened, he was heading from the airport to Bishkek too. He showed me the bus and we got on together. Only when we got off did I realise he had quietly paid my fare as well, without saying a word. That was the moment I met Kyrgyz hospitality.

A selfie with my new Kyrgyz friend on the bus from the airport into town

By then I'd had two good experiences: the friendly, book-reading passengers on the plane, and this young man who showed me the way and treated me without expecting anything in return. I saw the same thing again and again later in the trip: backpacking in Kyrgyzstan is easy thanks to the kindness and culture of its people. They help you with an open heart and answer any question you have.

Exploring Bishkek

Bishkek is a city where every step shows you traces of two eras: the heavy, grand buildings of the Soviet period, and the symbols that independent Kyrgyzstan has raised for its own identity. The streets are wide and full of trees, and when the sky is clear, the snowy peaks of the Ala-Too range appear behind the clouds.

Bishkek City Hall

The first building that caught my eye was Bishkek City Hall: a Soviet neoclassical building with six tall white columns, a blue pediment and the national emblem of Kyrgyzstan in the middle. The red flag of Kyrgyzstan with its yellow sun waved above it, and the blue spruces on either side completed the picture.

The National Philharmonic and the Manas monument

One of the most striking spots in the city is the square in front of the Kyrgyz National Philharmonic, named after Toktogul Satylganov, the great Kyrgyz composer and poet. In the middle of the square, Manas stands on top of a tall column: on horseback, sword in hand, fighting a dragon. On either side are two more statues: Kanykei, Manas's loyal wife, and Bakai, his wise counsellor.

Manas is the hero of the great Kyrgyz epic, an epic passed down by word of mouth through storytellers called manaschi, and counted among the longest epics in the world. For the Kyrgyz, Manas is something like Rostam, the hero of the Persian Shahnameh, is for us Iranians.

A statue of an akyn

In the nearby parks you can see stone statues of Kyrgyz poets and wandering musicians, known as akyns. One old man with a white beard and the traditional Kyrgyz felt hat, the kalpak, was staring seriously at the passers-by.

The White House

Bishkek's White House, or Ak Ui, is a huge marble building that houses the Kyrgyz presidency. It has played an important role in the country's recent history: in the revolutions of 2005 and 2010, protesters reached this very building and brought down governments. I stood in front of it too and took a photo as a souvenir.

Ala-Too Square and the equestrian Manas

The heart of the city is Ala-Too Square. In the middle stands another statue of Manas, this time on a calm and dignified horse, on a tall pedestal of red stone. Interestingly, this spot has changed hands several times: in Soviet times there was a statue of Lenin here, after independence it was replaced by the statue of Freedom (Erkindik), and in 2011 Manas took the pedestal. Those few changes tell the story of a whole country. That day the sky was full of scattered clouds, and the sunlight behind them turned the statue into a majestic silhouette.

Monument to the Fighters of the Revolution

A little further on is the Monument to the Fighters of the Revolution: a tall woman holding up a banner with one hand and stretching the other forward. Two groups of figures on either side show ordinary people, farmers and fighters. It is one of the landmark works of Soviet-era art in Bishkek.

Soviet-era sculptures

Another statue I saw was a group of men, women and children: a man with a child on his shoulder pointing towards the horizon, and beside him a young man and a girl. This style of sculpture, promising a bright future, is everywhere in Bishkek, a reminder of the decades when Kyrgyzstan was part of the Soviet Union.

The end of the first day

After walking around the city, I headed back to the hostel. Now that I had seen Bishkek, I was longing for Kyrgyz nature, so I looked around for tours and travel agencies that could take me into the mountains and to the lakes. In the evening I went out for dinner, to a restaurant with colourful sofas and fabrics full of traditional Central Asian patterns.

Dinner on the first night: beshbarmak and tea

What I ordered was beshbarmak, the national dish of Kyrgyzstan: wide homemade noodles in broth, with chopped boiled meat and onion, and on top a few round slices that I think were horse meat. It fascinated me, because in Iran we don't eat horse meat; but for the Kyrgyz, who have lived with horses for centuries, horse meat is part of the traditional table and of celebrations. A pot of hot tea came alongside it, so my first night in Bishkek ended warm.

Manas, the flag and the roof of the yurt

In just one day I had seen Manas again and again: on horseback in Ala-Too Square, fighting a dragon in front of the Philharmonic, and even in the name of Bishkek's international airport, which is called Manas. The Kyrgyz see the Manas epic as the backbone of their national identity. According to the epic, Manas was the hero who united forty scattered Kyrgyz tribes and turned them into one nation against their enemies.

The flag of Kyrgyzstan

You can see the traces of this story on the flag of Kyrgyzstan too. The red field stands for bravery and courage, and it is said that Manas's banner was red as well. In the middle shines a yellow sun with forty rays, each ray standing for one of the forty tribes that Manas united.

But the most interesting part is the design in the centre of the sun: a circle crossed by three lines from each side. This is the tunduk, the wooden ring at the top of the roof of a yurt, the traditional tent of Kyrgyz nomads. The tunduk is the opening that lets smoke out and light in. For Kyrgyz nomads, the tunduk is a symbol of home, family and the bond between generations, and it is passed down from father to son. And now that simple ring from the roof of a nomad's tent has made it to the heart of a country's flag. A few days later I spent a night in a yurt deep in the mountains, and that same tunduk was right above my head, the very design I had seen on the flag.

Day two: Bishkek's markets and neighbourhoods

I gave the second day to Bishkek again, but this time not to its squares and official buildings. Instead I went to the local markets and the neighbourhoods where people's real lives happen. For a backpacker, the market is the best place in town: it's cheap, full of colours and smells, and it's where you can talk to people.

The Holy Resurrection Cathedral in Bishkek

On the way I came across the Holy Resurrection Cathedral, a white and blue Orthodox church with golden and blue onion domes. The cathedral is a reminder of the Russian community that has stayed in Kyrgyzstan since the days of the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union, and it shows how comfortably this Muslim-majority country lives with religious diversity.

Inside the cathedral: murals of the Resurrection and golden icons

Inside the church it was quiet and cool. The wall behind the altar was covered with colourful paintings of the Resurrection, with two big red letters, "ХВ", standing for "Христос Воскресе", meaning "Christ is risen". Gold-framed icons and brass candlesticks made the space feel magnificent.

With one of my new Kyrgyz friends on the streets of Bishkek

In the markets and neighbourhoods I made new friends and got talking with them. Most Kyrgyz speak only a little English and conversation was sometimes hard, but Google Translate came to the rescue and we understood each other easily. In Kyrgyzstan you hear three languages side by side: Kyrgyz, Russian and, in tourist places, a bit of English. Knowing a few words of Russian is very useful here.

Finding a nature tour

In Bishkek there are lots of agencies running nature tours, and they're not hard to find at all. I picked one too: a two-day trip with one night sleeping in a yurt, a nomad's tent, around Lake Issyk-Kul.

The good thing about these tours is that you travel in minibuses with passengers from all over the world, so you end up with new travel companions. For those two days we shared the road with about twenty American students who had come to Kyrgyzstan to learn Russian. There were travellers from Russia too, and even Kyrgyz people who had come to see the nature of their own country. In the minibus, Russian, English and Kyrgyz all mixed together.

Issyk-Kul: the lake that never freezes

Issyk-Kul lies in eastern Kyrgyzstan, at about 1,600 metres above sea level, among the Tian Shan mountains. "Issyk-Kul" means "warm lake" in Kyrgyz, because its water is slightly salty and, despite the harsh winters of the region, it never freezes. One branch of the Silk Road ran along this lake, and local legends tell of cities that sank beneath its waters. Archaeologists have indeed found remains of ancient settlements on the lake bed.

Kyrgyzstan is called "the Switzerland of Asia", and once you leave the lakeside road and enter the valleys, you understand why: thick forests of Tian Shan spruce, roaring rivers, green meadows and snowy peaks peeking out from behind the clouds. Wild nature that is still untouched.

Fairy Tale Canyon (Skazka)

Skazka Canyon, with the blue of Lake Issyk-Kul on the horizon

One of our first stops on the southern shore of Issyk-Kul was Skazka Canyon. "Skazka" means "fairy tale" in Russian, and once you step inside you understand why it got that name. Hills and rocks of clay and sandstone, in shades of red, orange, yellow and pink, have been carved by wind and rain over millions of years into shapes in which everyone sees something different: a castle, a dragon, the Great Wall of China or a sleeping animal. Local people have even given names to some of the rocks.

Colourful, cracked grooves of earth in the heart of the canyon

From the top of the hills the view is stunning: dry red rocks in front of you, and behind them the endless blue of Issyk-Kul stretching to the horizon, with the snowy mountains on the far shore lost in the clouds. Seeing this red desert and that blue lake side by side is one of the strangest and most beautiful contrasts in Kyrgyz nature. Walking over the cracked earth and the colourful grooves of the canyon felt like walking on another planet.

A dirt road towards the snowy peaks; the sign for the Sharkyratma river

A roaring river between spruces and snowy peaks

A dirt road took us deep into the valley. By the road was a blue sign with the name of the Sharkyratma river, a name that in Kyrgyz refers to the roar of rushing water, and honestly, the river sounded exactly like that. Cold, foaming water came down from the snowy mountains and rushed between the rocks.

A green valley with a grazing cow and a snowy peak in the distance

Me in the valley, facing the snowy peaks

A group hike in the rain through the spruce forest, towards the waterfall

To reach the waterfall we had to climb a wet, rocky path through the spruce forest. A light rain was falling, and our group, with umbrellas and raincoats in yellow, pink and blue, made a line among the tall trees, a scene straight out of a postcard.

A waterfall between rocks and spruces

Me by the waterfall, in a raincoat making up for my summer clothes

Along the way we reached waterfalls pouring down over wet, moss-covered rocks. The weather was cloudy and damp, and that's where I realised the summer clothes I had packed for Beirut and Syria were no use here; a light raincoat was the only thing keeping me warm.

Jeti-Oguz: seven red bulls

The red rocks of Jeti-Oguz under a cloudy evening sky

One of the most amazing places we saw was the red rocks of Jeti-Oguz. Jeti-Oguz means "seven bulls" in Kyrgyz, and the name comes from the red sandstone rocks lined up side by side that from afar look like a herd of bulls. A local legend says that seven bulls were once turned to stone as punishment for one man's betrayal and greed. Near these rocks there is another one called "Broken Heart", with a sad love story behind it. In the cloudy evening light, the red of the rocks under a purple and grey sky made a view I will never forget.

A night in a yurt

The camp's yurts at the foot of the red rocks and the spruce forest

We spent the night in a yurt camp: round white tents with blue stripes and traditional patterns, at the foot of the red rocks and the spruce forest. As a backpacker, sleeping in a yurt was an unforgettable experience: simple, cosy, and close to the nomadic life that has gone on in these mountains for centuries. Above my head was that same tunduk, the ring I had seen on the flag of Kyrgyzstan.

Meadow and yurts on the mountainside

A valley full of spruces and scattered yurts

The yurt camp from above, with horses and snowy mountains in the distance

In the morning the summer pastures had come to life. Yurts were scattered across wide meadows, horses grazed freely and clouds drifted slowly over the peaks. Here, nomads still bring their herds up to the summer pastures and live in yurts, the same way of life the Kyrgyz have lived for centuries.

The hiking trail on the forested slope

A tall waterfall seen from below

After breakfast we set off on a hike along a muddy, green path on the forested slope, under the drooping branches of the spruces, until we reached a tall waterfall pouring down over dark rocks.

A green valley and a winding river, seen from high on the trail

From under the spruce branches: the yurt camp in the middle of the forest

An endless valley of spruce and meadow, all the way to the snowy peaks

The higher we climbed, the wider the view became. From the top of the trail we could see a valley with a river winding through it like a silver thread, yurts scattered like white seeds across the meadow, and spruce forest running all the way to the foot of the snowy peaks. The beauty and greenery there were endless; every bend in the path showed us a new view, and every time I thought it couldn't get more beautiful, I was wrong again.

Even though it was the middle of summer, when the heat in Iran is at its peak, the weather here was very cool and the nights got properly cold. Funnily enough, the European travellers spent the evenings comfortably in shorts with just a jacket, but for me, with so few warm clothes, the nights were so cold that I had to sleep under several blankets. That's when I appreciated my light raincoat more than ever and understood how unready the backpack I had packed for the heat of Beirut was for Kyrgyzstan.

The golden eagle, companion of Kyrgyz hunters

A golden eagle beside the yurts, at the foot of the red rocks

At one of the camps, at the foot of the red rocks, a big golden eagle sat on a wooden post, watching everything around it with a sharp eye. Hunting with eagles is one of the oldest traditions of the Kyrgyz nomads. Hunters who train eagles are called berkutchi. They take an eaglet from the mountains, train it patiently for years and hunt foxes and hares with it. The bond between hunter and eagle runs so deep that after a few years many berkutchi release their eagle back into the wild. Seeing this bird up close, next to the yurts and beneath the red rocks, reminded me of the same nomadic life I had seen on the flag and in the epic of Manas.

Goodbye, Issyk-Kul

The sandy shore of Issyk-Kul under a blue sky

The last stop of the tour was the shore of Issyk-Kul: soft sand, clear blue water, and a sky with feathery clouds stretching to the horizon. The lake is so big that you can't see the opposite shore, and it looks more like a sea than a lake. Some of our group went into the water, others sat on the sand and gazed at the horizon. It was the end of two days full of forests and waterfalls, red rocks, yurts and an eagle, and people from every corner of the world who had become fellow travellers in one minibus.
